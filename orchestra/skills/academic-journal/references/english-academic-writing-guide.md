## 1. Paragraph Development

A paragraph is not a bag of sentences on the same topic. It is a unit of argument. Each paragraph makes exactly one claim and supports it. The structure you choose depends on what that claim is and where in the paper it sits.

### 1.1 Four Paragraph Structures

**Structure A: Claim-First (Topic Sentence -> Evidence -> Analysis -> Bridge)**

The workhorse structure. The first sentence states the claim. The next sentences supply evidence (data, citations, reasoning). The second-to-last sentence analyzes what the evidence means. The final sentence bridges to the next paragraph.

*When to use:* Results sections, Methods justifications, anywhere you are presenting a finding or a methodological choice.

*Example:*
> Droplet size distributions shifted toward smaller diameters under increased shear (p < 0.001). At 500 s^-1, the median diameter decreased from 12.3 microm to 8.1 microm (95% CI [7.6, 8.6]), with an accompanying 40% increase in polydispersity index. This narrowing and broadening pattern is consistent with a tip-streaming mechanism rather than bulk fragmentation. To test this mechanism directly, we turned to high-speed imaging of single-drop breakup events.

**Structure B: Inverted Pyramid (Specific Finding -> Explanation -> Context)**

Lead with the specific result. Then explain it. Then connect it outward to the broader literature or framework. This structure gives primacy to your data.

*When to use:* Strongest for Results paragraphs where the finding is surprising, large in magnitude, or central to the paper's contribution.

*Example:*
> Treatment with compound A-7 reduced tumor volume by 68% relative to vehicle control (p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 1.9). This effect size is comparable to the current first-line therapy cisplatin (71% reduction in the same xenograft model; Johnson et al., 2022), but with a six-fold improvement in the therapeutic index. The finding positions A-7 as a candidate for tumors where cisplatin nephrotoxicity precludes adequate dosing.

**Structure C: Question-Driven (Pose Question -> Present Evidence -> Answer)**

Begin the paragraph with a genuine question. The subsequent sentences provide the evidence that answers it. The final sentence states the answer explicitly.

*When to use:* Distinctively human prose pattern. Best for Discussion sections where you are interpreting ambiguous results, exploring alternative explanations, or addressing counterarguments.

*Example:*
> Does the observed performance gain reflect genuine skill acquisition or mere familiarity with the test interface? The learning curve plateaued after session 3 in the experimental group but continued to rise through session 5 in controls, who used a different interface. If interface familiarity drove the effect, we would expect convergence once both groups had equal exposure. The persistent divergence suggests that the intervention produced transferable skill gains, not task-specific adaptation.

**Structure D: Narrative / Transition (Short, Structural Purpose)**

A brief paragraph (2-4 sentences) whose only job is to carry the reader from one major section or argument to the next. It does not introduce new claims or evidence.

*When to use:* Sparingly — at most 2-3 per paper. Use when the logical leap between sections is large enough to require a bridge sentence, never as a substitute for genuine flow.

*Example:*
> The results above establish that the intervention reduces error rates. Whether this reduction translates to clinically meaningful outcomes depends on the severity of errors prevented and the cost of the intervention itself. The next section addresses both questions.

### 1.2 Theme-Rheme Progression

Coherent paragraphs follow the given-before-new principle. Each sentence begins with information the reader already knows (the theme, usually carried from the previous sentence's rheme) and ends with new information (the rheme).

*Broken progression (theme shifts without carry-forward):*
> Droplet breakup occurs via capillary instability. The Reynolds number determines flow regime. Surfactants reduce interfacial tension.

*Repaired progression (each sentence's theme derives from the previous sentence's rheme):*
> Droplet breakup occurs via capillary instability. This instability is triggered when the capillary number exceeds a critical value, which depends on both the viscosity ratio and the flow type. The flow type, in turn, is determined by the Reynolds number of the continuous phase.

### 1.3 End-Weight

Place complex or heavy information at the end of the sentence. Readers parse sentence-final positions as the natural location for new, elaborated, or important content. Front-loading complexity forces the reader to hold too much in working memory.

*Weak (heavy subject):*
> The observation that participants in the high-anxiety condition who had previously undergone cognitive behavioral therapy performed better on the Stroop task than those who had not is notable.

*Strong (light subject, heavy predicate):*
> Participants in the high-anxiety condition performed better on the Stroop task if they had previously undergone cognitive behavioral therapy, an observation that is notable for two reasons.

### 1.4 Annotated Rewrite

**Original (common NNES problems: topic sentence is a truism, evidence is vague, no bridge):**
> Machine learning has been widely used in medical diagnosis in recent years. Many studies have applied deep learning to medical imaging tasks. CNNs have shown promising results for classification problems. Transformer-based models have also been explored.

**Rewrite (claim-first, evidence with specifics, analysis, bridge):**
> Deep learning now matches or exceeds radiologist performance on narrowly defined classification tasks, but generalizability across acquisition protocols remains a documented failure mode. In a multi-site benchmark of 14 CNN architectures, top-1 accuracy on in-distribution test sets averaged 0.94, but fell to 0.62 when models were evaluated on scanners from hospitals not represented in training data (Roberts et al., 2023). This 32-point gap is larger than the average gain from architecture improvements over the past three years (approximately 8 points), suggesting that data heterogeneity, not model capacity, is the binding constraint. Transformer-based architectures, which learn global feature dependencies, offer one path to closing this gap — but only if training corpora are constructed to represent the full range of acquisition variability.

*Changes annotated:*
1. Claim replaces vague truism ("widely used") with specific argument ("matches or exceeds, but fails on generalizability").
2. Evidence provides exact numbers (0.94, 0.62, 32-point gap) instead of "promising results."
3. Analysis interprets what the numbers mean (data heterogeneity is the binding constraint).
4. Bridge names the next topic (transformers) but ties it to the current argument (heterogeneity).

## 2. Evidence-Based Argumentation

Academic writing earns assertions through evidence, not through vocabulary. The sentence-level formula is: **CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> ANALYSIS -> LINK**. Every paragraph should trace this arc at least once.

### 2.1 Claims with Precision

Replace qualitative intensifiers with quantitative specifics. "Significantly improved" is not a finding; it is a placeholder. Provide the number, the confidence interval, the effect size, the exact p-value, or a specific reference.

| Weak (adjective-reliant) | Strong (number-backed) |
|---|---|
| X showed significant improvement | X improved accuracy by 12.3 percentage points (95% CI [9.1, 15.5], p = 0.003) |
| The treatment was highly effective | The treatment reduced symptom severity by 2.4 standard deviations relative to waitlist control (Cohen's d = 1.3) |
| There was a strong correlation | Annual rainfall and crop yield were correlated at r = 0.73 (p < 0.001, n = 348 county-years) |
| Most participants improved | 78 of 94 participants (83%) showed improvement exceeding the minimal clinically important difference of 4 points |
| Performance degraded substantially | Throughput fell from 1,240 req/s to 340 req/s under adversarial traffic — a 3.6x degradation |

### 2.2 Evidence Quality Signal

Not all evidence is equal. Signal the quality of your evidence through word choice:

- **Demonstrate / establish / confirm**: reserved for converging evidence across multiple methods or pre-registered replications. Do not use for a single exploratory analysis.
- **Show / find / report**: standard for primary results of the current study.
- **Suggest / indicate / point to**: for results that support but do not settle a question — typically exploratory analyses, subgroup results with wide confidence intervals, or correlational patterns.
- **Consistent with / compatible with**: for results that align with an existing theory but do not distinguish it from alternatives.

### 2.3 Transitioning from Evidence to Interpretation

The most common overclaiming pattern in NNES writing is a smooth rhetorical jump from evidence to strong interpretation with no hedging or qualification. Insert a reasoning step.

*Overclaiming (evidence-to-conclusion in one leap):*
> Participants in the mindfulness condition reported 23% lower anxiety scores (p = 0.02). Therefore, mindfulness causes reductions in anxiety.

*Appropriate (evidence-to-interpretation with reasoning):*
> Participants in the mindfulness condition reported 23% lower anxiety scores (p = 0.02). This is consistent with an attentional reallocation account, in which mindfulness training reduces the cognitive resources available for threat monitoring. However, because the control condition was a passive waitlist, non-specific effects of group participation cannot be ruled out. An active control is needed to isolate the specific effect of mindfulness training.

### 2.4 The "One Claim, One Evidence Source" Principle

Each paragraph should make one central claim and marshal the evidence that supports it. If you find yourself stacking three citations in one parenthetical `(Smith, 2019; Jones, 2020; Lee, 2021)`, you are either (a) listing rather than arguing, or (b) providing background that belongs in the Introduction. One citation per claim, unless you are genuinely synthesizing a method or finding that multiple groups independently established.

## 3. Citation Integration

Citations are not decoration. They are arguments about intellectual lineage. How you cite — the reporting verb, the syntactic position, the company a citation keeps — signals what you think about the work you are citing.

### 3.1 Integral vs. Non-Integral Citation

**Integral citation** (author name in the sentence): The cited work is the grammatical subject or agent of your sentence. Use this when the author's identity matters — a key predecessor, a contrasting finding, a methodological choice you are adopting or rejecting.

> Smith et al. (2020) found that dose-response linearity breaks down below 0.1 mg/kg, a threshold we adopted in our low-dose condition.

**Non-integral citation** (parenthetical only): The cited work supports your claim without invoking the author as an agent. Use this for established facts, background information, and supporting evidence where no single paper is pivotal.

> Dose-response linearity breaks down below 0.1 mg/kg (Smith et al., 2020), motivating our selection of 0.05 mg/kg as the low-dose condition.

**Target ratio:** At least 30% of citations should be integral (author-name-in-text). A manuscript where every citation is parenthetical reads as if the author has no relationship with the literature beyond keyword search.

### 3.2 Reporting Verb Classification

Choose reporting verbs that accurately convey your stance toward the cited work.

**ARGUE group** (the cited author is making a claim, not reporting a settled fact): *argue, contend, posit, propose, theorize, advance the view that, maintain, hold*

> Chen and Rivera (2021) argue that attentional bias modification works not by retraining attention but by strengthening top-down control, a position challenged by null results in pre-registered replications (Lopez, 2023).

**FIND group** (the cited author reports empirical results): *demonstrate, show, report, establish, confirm, document, reveal, observe*

> Using a within-subjects crossover design, Okonkwo et al. (2022) demonstrated that the effect size doubles when feedback is delivered within 200 ms of the response.

**SUGGEST group** (the cited evidence is suggestive but not conclusive): *suggest, indicate, imply, point to, hint at, raise the possibility*

> Post-hoc analyses suggested that the null result in the full sample may mask a large effect in participants scoring above the median on baseline working memory (Park et al., 2021).

**OBSERVE group** (the cited author detected or identified something): *observe, note, detect, identify, find, record, document*

> Liu and Zhang (2019) observed an unexpected U-shaped relationship between dosage and response latency, which our study was designed to explain.

### 3.3 Citation Density

- **One citation per claim** is the default. If a finding is truly established, one authoritative reference suffices.
- **Maximum 3 citations per parenthetical.** Beyond three, the reader cannot track who contributes what. If you genuinely need to cite six papers establishing a finding, write: "This finding is well-established (see Smith, 2019, for a review; subsequent replications include Jones, 2020; Lee, 2021; and Patel, 2022)."
- **Citation stacking** — `(Author A, year; Author B, year; Author C, year; Author D, year)` — is a distinctive LLM pattern. Human authors cite selectively.

### 3.4 Tense in Citation Reporting

- **Past tense** for findings of specific studies: "Smith et al. (2020) found that..."
- **Present perfect** for a body of work with ongoing relevance: "Several groups have reported that..."
- **Present tense** for currently accepted facts or theories: "The dual-process model holds that..."

## 4. Rhetorical Moves in Discussion

The Discussion is where your paper earns its claim on the reader's attention. It is not a recap of Results. It is an argument about what your results mean, how they relate to what others have found, and why the reader should update their beliefs.

### 4.1 Answer First

Open the Discussion with a direct, substantive answer to the research question. Do not clear your throat. Do not summarize what you did. Do not write a bridging sentence about the importance of your topic. The reader finished your Results and wants to know: what does this mean?

*Weak opening:*
> This study investigated the relationship between sleep duration and cognitive performance in older adults. The findings contribute to the growing literature on sleep and aging.

*Strong opening:*
> Sleep durations below 6 hours and above 9 hours were both associated with cognitive decline over the two-year follow-up, with the 6-to-8-hour range showing the shallowest decline trajectory. This U-shaped relationship, which persisted after adjusting for baseline cognitive status and nine potential confounders, suggests that sleep duration operates as both a risk marker and a potential intervention target.

### 4.2 Contrast Structures

Use explicit contrast language to position your findings against the literature. Ambiguous relationships ("consistent with prior work in some respects but different in others") force the reader to do the analytical work you should have done.

**Template A — Direct contradiction:**
> Unlike Rodriguez et al. (2019), who found that the effect disappeared when controlling for socioeconomic status, our results show a persistent gap (d = 0.34) after matching on the same covariates. The divergence likely reflects differences in measurement: Rodriguez used a binary SES indicator, while we used a composite index with finer granularity.

**Template B — Partial consistency with extension:**
> Whereas Kumar and Singh (2020) demonstrated the effect in laboratory settings with undergraduate participants, our field experiment extends the finding to a working professional sample under naturalistic conditions.

**Template C — Field split characterization:**
> The literature on this question is divided. Cross-sectional studies consistently report a positive association, but the two existing longitudinal studies (Tan, 2018; Williams, 2021) find null results. Our three-wave panel data with a five-year interval shows that the association is positive at wave 1 but attenuates to zero by wave 3, suggesting that the cross-sectional findings may reflect reverse causality.

### 4.3 Concession and Qualification

Good academic writing acknowledges uncertainty without deflating its own argument. Concede specific limitations; do not apologize for the entire enterprise.

*Weak concession (generic self-undermining):*
> This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small. Future research should replicate these findings with larger samples.

*Strong concession (specific limitation with interpretation):*
> The sample size (n = 84) is sufficient to detect medium-to-large effects (d >= 0.45 at 80% power) but underpowered for the small effect sizes (d < 0.2) that are typical of interaction effects in this literature. Consequently, our null result for the gender-by-treatment interaction should be interpreted as absence of evidence for a large interaction, not evidence of absence.

**Qualification template (replaces "more research is needed"):**
> While this does not conclusively establish causation, the temporal ordering (exposure preceded outcome by a median of 4.2 years) and the dose-response gradient (risk increased monotonically across quartiles) strengthen the case beyond what cross-sectional designs can provide.

> The generalizability of this finding is constrained by the single-site design. Multi-site replication with diverse populations would test whether the effect size is consistent across health systems with different patient demographics and care protocols.

### 4.4 Limitations Contextualized, Not Ghettoed

Do not collect all limitations into a final paragraph labeled "Limitations." Each limitation should appear where it is contextually relevant — the measurement limitation beside the finding it affects, the design limitation beside the conclusion it constrains. If you must have a Limitations subsection, keep it to genuinely cross-cutting issues (single-site design, measurement timing) that cannot be addressed alongside specific findings.

## 5. Nominal Phrase Complexity

Noun phrases are the engine of academic English — they carry the concepts, variables, and entities your paper is about. But their length and complexity must be calibrated to the reader's cognitive load at each point in the paper.

### 5.1 When to Expand Noun Phrases

Expand noun phrases when you are **introducing a key concept for the first time** or when the concept is **central enough to deserve precise specification**. A well-constructed complex noun phrase eliminates ambiguity.

> *Thermal boundary layer thickness at the stagnation point of a hypersonic blunt-body flow* is acceptable in a Methods section because it precisely specifies a construct the reader needs to understand the subsequent analysis.

### 5.2 When to Simplify

Simplify noun phrases in **transition sentences, methods descriptions, and results reporting** — anywhere the reader is processing information under cognitive load. The reader who is tracking a statistical procedure or a chain of causal logic should not also be parsing a four-line noun phrase.

*Over-compressed (triple-stacked pre-modifiers):*
> the hospital-acquired infection rate reduction intervention compliance assessment protocol

*Restored (post-modification with prepositions):*
> the protocol for assessing compliance with the intervention designed to reduce hospital-acquired infection rates

### 5.3 Common Pitfalls for Chinese NNES Authors

**Over-long of-phrase chains.** English can tolerate two nested "of" constructions; three is borderline; four is unreadable.

*Problematic:*
> the effect of the concentration of the inhibitor of the enzyme on the rate of the reaction

*Repaired:*
> how inhibitor concentration affects reaction rate (split into what the variable is and what it does)

**Missing articles in complex noun phrases.** When a noun phrase contains multiple pre-modifiers and post-modifiers, Chinese NNES authors often drop articles because Chinese lacks an equivalent feature.

*Incorrect:* "Effect of temperature on reaction rate was significant."
*Correct:* "The effect of temperature on the reaction rate was significant."

*Rule of thumb:* If the head noun has been referenced before or is specified by a post-modifying phrase (of-phrase, that-clause), it requires "the."

**Stacked pre-modifiers without hyphenation.** English uses hyphenation to signal that a sequence of words functions as a single adjective.

*Ambiguous:* "small molecule binding affinity assay"
*Clarified:* "small-molecule binding-affinity assay" (though here, a prepositional post-modifier is better: "assay of binding affinity for small molecules")

## 6. Discipline Quick-Reference

Each discipline has an argumentation skeleton that reviewers expect. Match your paper's structure to your field's conventions. These are extracted from `discipline-writing-patterns.md` and the De-AI detection framework Part 2.

### 6.1 CS / Engineering

**Skeleton:** System -> Implementation -> Evaluation -> Ablation -> Limitations

- The "system" section replaces a traditional Methods section. Describe architecture, algorithms, and design choices.
- **Reproducibility = code availability.** Without a public repository or artifact, claims are unverifiable. Provide exact software versions, package dependencies, random seeds, and hardware specifications.
- **Ablation studies are mandatory.** Readers expect you to remove components one at a time and show that each matters. A table reporting a single model's performance is not an evaluation — it is a demo.
- Architecture diagrams carry argumentative weight. A well-labeled figure with component-level annotations replaces paragraphs of prose.

### 6.2 Biomedicine

**Skeleton:** Mechanism -> In vitro -> In vivo -> Clinical relevance -> Limitations

- **Hierarchy of evidence:** in vivo > in vitro. A pure in vitro paper has a lower venue ceiling. Specify how in vitro findings relate to in vivo plausibility.
- **Mechanism requires manipulation.** "We observed that A correlates with B" is not a mechanism. To claim mechanism, you must experimentally manipulate A and show B changes as predicted.
- Report exact N, exact doses, exact durations, and exact assay conditions. "Cells were treated with compound X" is insufficient — "HeLa cells (ATCC CCL-2, passage 4-8) were treated with 10 nM compound X (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS 123-45-6) for 24 h at 37 C, 5% CO2."

### 6.3 Social Science

**Skeleton:** Theory -> Research Design -> Main Effects -> Heterogeneity -> Mechanisms

- **Identification strategy is the centerpiece.** "We controlled for income and education" is not a strategy. State the source of identifying variation (natural experiment, panel structure, instrumental variable, regression discontinuity) and provide the tests that support its validity.
- **Robustness checks are expected.** Alternative specifications, sub-sample analyses, placebo tests, different operationalizations of the dependent variable.
- **Endogeneity is the default reviewer concern.** Every observational finding faces "but what about unobserved confounds?" Address it preemptively with the best strategy your data allows.

### 6.4 Economics

**Skeleton:** Motivation -> Institutional Context -> Data -> Identification -> Main Results -> Robustness -> Mechanisms -> Policy Implications

- The identification section can be as long as the results section. This is a feature, not a bug.
- Present balance tables (covariate means by treatment status) and first-stage results for IV designs.
- **Weak instrument (F < 10) or parallel trends violation** are the most common rejection reasons.
- Report standard errors clustered at the level of treatment assignment, not at the observation level.

### 6.5 Environmental / Geoscience

**Skeleton:** Phenomenon Description -> Data Sources -> Spatial/Temporal Analysis -> Attribution -> Implications -> Limitations

- Maps and time series are primary evidence, not illustrations. Annotate them with statistical results.
- Disaggregate spatial and temporal variation — confounding between the two is a common rejection reason.

## 7. Section Writing Protocols

### 7.1 Abstract

The Abstract is the most-read section of your paper and the last one you should write. It must work as a standalone text.

**Four-sentence model:**
1. **What is known** (1 sentence): The established fact or consensus position that your paper builds on or challenges.
2. **What is unknown** (1 sentence): The gap, puzzle, or contradiction you address.
3. **What we did** (1-2 sentences): Your approach — study design, sample, key method.
4. **What we found** (1-2 sentences): Your main result with at least one specific number (effect size, confidence interval, or exact p-value).

**Five-sentence model** (adds implications):
5. **What it means** (1 sentence): One specific implication — for theory, practice, or policy.

**Hard requirements:**
- Target 150-250 words (check your target journal; some cap at 150).
- Must contain at least one specific number.
- Must state the finding, not just the activity ("We investigated..." is not enough; "We found that..." is).
- No citations (unless the journal permits; most do not).
- No abbreviations without definition.

*Example (biomedicine, 4-sentence model):*
> Physical activity is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality in observational cohorts, but whether this association is causal and whether the dose-response relationship is linear remain debated. We conducted a Mendelian randomization study using genetic variants associated with accelerometer-measured physical activity as instrumental variables in 377,234 UK Biobank participants. Each additional 30 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day was associated with a 23% reduction in cardiovascular mortality risk (HR 0.77, 95% CI [0.69, 0.86], p < 0.001), with no evidence of a plateau at high activity levels. These findings support a causal interpretation of the observational evidence and suggest that even modest increases in activity confer substantial benefit.

### 7.2 Introduction

A tight three-paragraph funnel. No roadmap paragraph. No more than 3 paragraphs without a break.

**Paragraph 1: Broad importance (1-2 sentences only)**

State what is known and why anyone should care. Do not write a mini-review. Do not start with "In recent years..." or "With the development of..."

*Acceptable:*
> Surface water salinization threatens drinking water supplies for over 300 million people globally, yet the mechanisms driving salt mobilization in freshwater systems remain poorly characterized.

*Unacceptable:*
> Water is essential for human life and economic development. In recent years, with the rapid development of industrialization and urbanization, water quality has become an increasingly important issue worldwide.

**Paragraph 2: Specific gap with named citations**

Identify the specific gap, contradiction, or open question your paper addresses. Use author-name-in-text citations. Do not say "few studies have examined..." without naming which studies exist and what they left undone.

*Acceptable:*
> While laboratory studies have established that chloride ions mobilize heavy metals from sediments under acidic conditions (Bower et al., 2018; Chen, 2020), field-scale evidence is limited to three single-watershed studies, all in temperate climates (Nguyen, 2019; Schmidt, 2021; Ito and Park, 2022). Whether the same mechanism operates in tropical watersheds with distinct hydrology and sediment chemistry is unknown.

**Paragraph 3: Your question + your approach + contribution statement**

State your research question explicitly. Describe your approach in one sentence. End with a contribution statement: what the reader will gain that they did not have before.

*Acceptable:*
> We tested whether chloride-driven metal mobilization operates in tropical watersheds by measuring sediment pore-water chemistry, stream metal concentrations, and chloride loads across 12 watersheds in the Mekong basin over a two-year period. The results establish that the mobilization mechanism generalizes to tropical systems but identify a previously unrecognized buffering role of iron oxyhydroxide-rich sediments that moderates the effect.

**Do NOT end the Introduction with a roadmap paragraph.** "The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the data and methods, Section 3 presents the results..." is a distinctive LLM pattern and has been out of fashion in human academic writing since approximately 2010. The paper's section headings are the roadmap.

### 7.3 Methods

**Tense:** Past.

**Goal:** Enough detail for replication. A reader with the same materials should be able to reproduce your procedure without guessing.

**Critical addition: "why" clauses.** Every major methodological choice should be accompanied by a brief justification. This is what distinguishes a protocol from a paper.

*Without "why" clause:*
> We used a random forest classifier with 500 trees.

*With "why" clause:*
> We used a random forest classifier with 500 trees because the feature space (n = 1,247) exceeded the sample size (n = 340), a regime in which random forests are less prone to overfitting than logistic regression or SVM.

**Include:**
- Software names, versions, and packages (e.g., "scikit-learn 1.3.2")
- Equipment specifications (manufacturer, model, city/country)
- Rejected alternatives if informative (e.g., "We did not use propensity score matching because the treatment-assignment model contained unverifiable exclusion restrictions.")
- Sample size justification (power analysis, feasibility constraint, or precedent)
- Pre-registration statement if applicable
- Ethics approval identifiers

**Minimum count:** At least 3 "why" clauses explaining methodological choices.

### 7.4 Results

**Tense:** Past.

**One finding per paragraph.** A Results paragraph should open with the finding, not the figure reference.

*Weak (figure-led):*
> Figure 3 shows the reaction rates as a function of temperature. As can be seen, the rate increases with temperature up to 60 C, after which it declines.

*Strong (finding-led):*
> Reaction rate increased linearly with temperature from 20 C to 60 C (r = 0.94, p < 0.001) but declined sharply above 60 C, falling to 34% of the peak rate at 80 C. This inverted-U pattern is consistent with a two-step mechanism in which thermal activation dominates below 60 C and enzyme denaturation dominates above it.

**Every finding needs:** effect size AND p-value AND confidence interval (where applicable). Never report "significant" without quantifying how large the effect is and how precise the estimate.

**Statistical reporting checklist for each result:**
- Test statistic (t, F, chi-squared, etc.) with degrees of freedom
- Exact p-value (not "p < 0.05" unless truly minuscule; report "p = 0.032")
- Effect size (Cohen's d, eta-squared, odds ratio, hazard ratio, etc.)
- Confidence interval for the primary effect size
- Sample size for that specific analysis (accounts for missing data)

### 7.5 Discussion

**Tense:** Mixed. Present for interpretations and claims about your data's meaning. Past for references to specific findings or previous studies.

**Structural rules:**
1. **Paragraph 1: Answer the research question.** Direct, no throat-clearing. (See Section 4.1.)
2. **Middle paragraphs: Compare to literature.** For each major finding, state how it aligns with or diverges from prior work. Use the contrast structures in Section 4.2. If a finding is unexpected, explain it — do not just note it is unexpected.
3. **Limitations appear contextually.** Each limitation sits beside the finding or interpretation it constrains. (See Section 4.4.)
4. **Final paragraph: Implications.** What should the reader do differently after reading your paper? Be specific. Do not say "these findings have important implications for policy." Say which policy, what change, and why your evidence supports it.

**Do NOT:**
- Restate Results. The reader just read them.
- Say "more research is needed" without specifying exactly what research, using what design, and why it would resolve the open question.
- Overclaim. "Prove," "conclusively demonstrate," and "establish beyond doubt" almost never apply to a single study.
- End with a generic call for future work. Specific future work is fine: "A randomized trial with an active control would test whether the effect is specific to the mindfulness component or shared across relaxation-based interventions."

## 8. Enhanced Writing Checklist

Run this 21-item checklist before submitting. It combines quality assurance with De-AI prevention, adapted from the English De-AI detection framework.

### Paragraph-Level

1. Every paragraph has a clear topic sentence that makes a claim — not a truism, not a topic announcement, not a meta-comment about what the paragraph will discuss.
2. Every claim is backed by evidence: a number, a confidence interval, a citation, or a logical argument. No bare assertions.
3. No paragraph exceeds 5 of the AI-associated lexical items from Tier 1-3 of the De-AI framework (delve, pivotal, tapestry, realm, foster, intricate).
4. "Moreover" = 0 instances regardless of paper length.
5. "Not only...but also..." = 0 instances.

### Structure-Level

6. No roadmap paragraph ("The remainder of this paper is organized as follows...").
7. Sentence-length standard deviation > 10 in at least 3 of 5 major sections (check: do you have any sentences under 8 words? Any over 35?).
8. Discourse markers within acceptable budget:
   - 3,000-word paper: 12-18 markers
   - 6,000-word paper: 20-35 markers
   - 10,000-word paper: 35-55 markers
   (Derived from `english-de-ai-quick-ref.md` D2 thresholds.)
9. At least 30% of citations are integral (author-name-in-text format).
10. No citation stacking: maximum 3 citations per parenthetical.

### Section-Specific

11. Methods contains at least 3 "why" clauses explaining methodological choices.
12. Results leads paragraphs with findings, not figure references ("Figure 3 shows...").
13. Discussion opens with a direct answer to the research question — no throat-clearing.
14. Limitations appear contextually within the Discussion flow, not ghettoed in a final paragraph.
15. Abstract contains at least one specific number (effect size, CI, exact p-value, or N).

### Mechanics

16. Every abbreviation is defined on first use in the main text (Abstract counts as a separate document for this purpose).
17. Terminology is consistent: one term per concept across all sections. Do not use "participants," "subjects," and "respondents" interchangeably.
18. Tense consistency:
    - Methods/Results: past tense.
    - Introduction: present tense (for established facts) and past (for specific prior studies).
    - Discussion: present for interpretations, past for referencing your own or others' specific findings.
19. "In the context of," "in terms of," and "with respect to" have been tested for deletion: if the sentence works without them, remove them.
20. The conclusion specifies exactly what research is needed, why, and ideally how it would be designed — not a generic call for future work.

### Readability

21. Paper reads aloud without metronome rhythm — varied sentence lengths, varied paragraph structures (not all claim-first, not all inverted pyramid, and never a series of sandwich paragraphs).
