# Composer: Discussion Section Writing

**Purpose:** Write a Discussion section that interprets results, compares to literature, and states limitations/implications — not a restatement of Results.
**Used by:** S4 (Journal), C3 (Coursework)
**Parameters:** None (convention-driven; word count determined by journal/course constraints)

## Instructions

1. **Open with the answer.** The first paragraph should directly answer the research question posed in the Introduction. Do not warm up with background restatement.
   - GOOD: "The central finding of this study is that X achieves Y, consistent with the hypothesis that Z."
   - BAD: "In this study, we investigated X using method Y. The results showed that..."

2. **Compare to literature, don't just cite it.** For each finding, explicitly state whether prior work agrees, disagrees, or is extended. Every citation should carry a relationship:
   - Agreement: "This aligns with Smith et al. (2020), who found..."
   - Disagreement: "In contrast to Jones et al. (2019), our results showed..."
   - Extension: "While prior work focused on domain A, our results extend this to domain B."

3. **Address unexpected results.** If a result contradicted your hypothesis, discuss why. Possible causes: methodological differences, confounding variables, insufficient power. Do not ignore or gloss over unexpected findings.

4. **Limitations, not as an afterthought.** Limitations should be a substantive subsection or paragraph, not a single sentence before "future work." For each limitation, state its impact on interpretation and what you did (or could not do) to mitigate it.

5. **Implications and significance.** State the broader significance of your findings. Who should care and why? This connects back to the "broad importance" from the Introduction funnel.

6. **Future work — specific and justified.** "More research is needed" is insufficient. Specify: what kind of study, on what population/dataset, using what approach, and why that specific next step follows from your results.

7. **No restated Results.** Do not re-report the numbers from the Results section. Reference findings conceptually ("the accuracy improvement," "the cross-domain advantage") and focus on what they mean, not what they are.

## Verification

- [ ] First paragraph answers the research question directly (no throat-clearing)
- [ ] Every citation to prior work includes an agree/disagree/extend relationship
- [ ] Unexpected results are addressed, not buried or omitted
- [ ] Limitations section discusses impact and mitigation, not just existence
- [ ] Future work specifies direction and rationale ("a longitudinal study in clinical settings would test whether...")
- [ ] No new results introduced — Discussion interprets, does not report
- [ ] No purely descriptive restatement of Results findings

## Common Pitfalls

- **Discussion becomes restated Results.** If you can replace a Discussion paragraph's first sentence with "In this study, we found that..." and the paragraph still makes sense, you are restating. Each paragraph must add interpretation, judgment, or connection to the broader literature.
- **Limitations buried in the last paragraph as an afterthought.** A single sentence before "future work" suggests the authors are hiding weaknesses. Limitations belong in their own substantive space.
- **"More research is needed" without specification.** Every reader knows more research is always needed. Tell them what kind, why, and what specific gap it would address.
- **Overclaiming.** "This solves the problem" when your study had 3 participants, or was on MNIST, or used synthetic data. Match the claim to the scope of evidence.
- **No funnel closure.** The Introduction opened broad and narrowed to a question. The Discussion should widen back out: specific answer → implications → future directions.
